# House style

Every article follows these rules. The writer, the reviser and the judges all read this file.

## Voice
- Short punchy sentences. Vary the rhythm on purpose. Never three sentences of the same length in a row.
- Simple words that everyday readers understand. No jargon without a one line explanation.
- Active voice only. Name who did what.
- Take a position when the facts support one. Do not hedge. If a fact is unconfirmed, say who claims it and that it is unconfirmed. That is not hedging, that is reporting.
- Lead with the specific: a number, a name, a place, a time. Then draw the wider point.
- One line per article may be slightly casual. Never unprofessional.

## Punctuation
- No hyphens or dashes inside sentences. Rewrite the sentence instead. Compound words that need a hyphen in standard spelling (well-known names, technical terms) are the only exception.
- No em dashes. No semicolons. No exclamation marks.
- No parentheses. Use a new sentence.

## Banned phrases
Never use any of these, in any form:
"It is worth noting", "This underscores", "This highlights", "In today's rapidly evolving landscape",
"It is important to understand", "This serves as a reminder", "Navigating the complexities of",
"In conclusion", "At the end of the day", "Game-changer", "Delve into", "Leverage" as a verb,
"Robust", "Seamless", "Unprecedented" unless literally without precedent and verified,
"Shed light on", "Moving forward", "It goes without saying", "Furthermore", "Moreover",
"Additionally", "In summary", and anything that reads like a consultant wrote it to sound safe.

## Structure
- Headline: under 90 characters. Specific. A real claim or a real number beats a teaser. No clickbait that the body cannot cash.
- Dek: one sentence, under 160 characters, adds new information the headline does not have.
- Body: 350 to 700 words. Open with the strongest verified fact. Explain why it matters to a reader in Nepal or in the diaspora by the third paragraph. Close on what happens next, with a date if one exists.
- Use `##` subheadings only when the piece runs past 450 words.
- Every number, quote and claim traces to a named source that appears in the sources list.
- Quote people in their own words. Attribute every quote.

## Accuracy and fairness
- Nothing invented. No composite quotes. No "sources say" without saying which outlet reported it.
- When outlets disagree, report the disagreement.
- Private individuals get privacy. Public officials get scrutiny.
- Allegations are allegations until a court or a documented record says otherwise.

## Chronology and dates
- Dates are calendar dates. "Wednesday" becomes "Wednesday, June 10". "In March" becomes "on March 2" when the date is verified. A dated record reads as evidence; an undated one reads as memory.
- Verify every date against the source's own timestamp. An event late at night that crosses midnight belongs to the next day.
- Time jumps are signposted: "Three months earlier." "Two days after that ruling." Every gap between two events is bridged or explained.
- Before an action is framed as a choice, confirm the sequence allows it. An action that follows a court order or a constitutional deadline is not a strategic move.

## Completeness
- Who, what, when, where, why. All five in every piece. No assumed prior knowledge.
- First mention of a person carries their role: "Rabi Lamichhane, the former Home Minister". Every time, even for famous names. The diaspora reader may not know them.
- First mention of a body or acronym carries a plain phrase: "the NHRC, Nepal's national human rights commission".
- Money carries USD context beside NPR or INR.
- The zero knowledge test: a reader with no background must be able to retell the story accurately after one read.

## Legal protection
- Never assert guilt. Show the record and let the reader draw the conclusion.
- Contested conduct is attributed to the outlet or body that alleged it, never stated in the newsroom's own voice: "Ratopati reported", "the NHRC named", "court filings show".
- Legal status is precise and re-verified before every piece that touches it. "Under investigation, released on bail, not charged" is not "faces prosecution".
- A subject's on record denial or response is always included when one exists. A documented attempt to reach the subject is noted when one was made.
- The line between what the evidence proves and what it suggests is drawn in plain words, every time.

## Editorial line
- The monarchy: never attack the king or the monarchy of Nepal. Cover supportively where the verified record allows. When the record cuts against the monarchy, skip the story rather than spin it. The record is never sharpened in either direction. The choice is coverage or silence, never distortion.

## The card headline (image_headline)
- It circulates alone as a screenshot, without the caption. It must survive alone.
- For contested or alleged conduct it carries a status signal (ACCUSED, ALLEGED, UNDER INVESTIGATION), or names the accuser ("NHRC NAMES OLI"), or states only the proven action. A genuinely open question is allowed only when the answer is genuinely open.
- A fully verified fact, a vote, a court order, a death toll, a filing, may run declarative. Never an unproven conduct claim in declarative voice.
- Two lines of five to six words, neither longer than 24 characters including spaces. No colon. It names the subject and carries the buried angle: who they are, what was happening, what happened, the detail that makes it unforgivable.

## The Facebook caption (caption)
- The card carries the hook and the sources. The caption carries the depth. Never duplicate the card's headline sentence in the hook.
- Hook: one or two lines, the most explosive verified fact in plain language, standing alone. Most readers never tap See more. Never open with an attribution verb; open with the consequence, the number, the damage.
- Body: 80 to 150 words for news, up to 300 for an investigation. Completeness overrides the ceiling; padding never does. Short paragraphs separated by blank lines. No markdown, no bullets, no numbered lists. Never end on a hedge; land on the sharpest established fact.
- Trigger: one line that creates friction by content alone. A side picking question with no obvious answer, a falsifiable prediction with a date, a statement that invites pushback. If ninety percent of readers would answer the same way at once, rewrite it. No "comment below", no tag prompts, no vote baiting. Two posts in five may leave it empty and close on the fact.
- Never in the caption: "Sources available in graphic", "Follow Nepal Wire" or hashtags. The newsroom adds the close.
